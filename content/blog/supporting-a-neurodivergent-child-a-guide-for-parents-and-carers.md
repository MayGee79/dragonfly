Parenting a neurodivergent child is a learning curve that nobody prepares you for. Whether your child has just been diagnosed, is in the process of being assessed, or you are simply noticing that they seem to experience the world differently from their peers, the questions multiply faster than the answers.

Am I doing enough? Am I doing too much? Should I push them to fit in or protect them from a world that does not accommodate them? How do I support them without smothering them? How do I advocate for them without fighting everyone? And when do I stop researching and just be their parent?

First, a reassurance: the fact that you are asking these questions means you are already doing something right. Many neurodivergent adults describe their childhood experience as one of not being understood. You are trying to understand. That is significant.

Let me work through some of the most common areas where parents appreciate guidance. 

Assessment and diagnosis: if you suspect your child may be neurodivergent, the first step is usually your GP, who can refer to CAMHS or a developmental paediatrics service. Be aware that waiting times for NHS assessment can be very long - in some areas, years. Private assessment is an option if it is financially accessible. A diagnosis is not a label - it is a key that unlocks understanding, support, and legal protections. Some families choose not to pursue formal diagnosis, and that is a valid choice too. What matters is understanding your child's needs, with or without a diagnostic label.

School support: I covered this in detail in the Young People series (the blog on neurodivergent young people at school), but the key points bear repeating. Your child may be entitled to reasonable adjustments under the Equality Act. For more significant needs, an EHCP provides legal protection. Communicate with school in writing, with specific requests. Attend every meeting. And remember that you are not asking for special treatment - you are asking for equitable access.

Emotional regulation: Neurodivergent children often have more intense emotional responses and less capacity to regulate them independently. This is neurological, not behavioural. Punishing a meltdown is like punishing a seizure - the child is not choosing the response. What helps is co-regulation: your calm presence, simple language, reduced demands, and time. After the meltdown, when the nervous system has settled, you can talk about what happened and explore strategies for next time. During the meltdown, your job is to keep them safe and be a calm anchor.

Meltdowns and shutdowns are not the same thing. Meltdowns are an outward explosion of overwhelm - crying, screaming, physical agitation. Shutdowns are an inward collapse - going quiet, withdrawing, going blank. Both are nervous system responses to exceeding capacity. Both need compassion, not consequences.

Talk to your child about their brain. Use age-appropriate, affirming language. 'Your brain works differently from some other brains, and that is not a bad thing - it is just different. Some things will be easier for you and some things will be harder, and we will figure out together what helps.' Avoid language that frames their neurodivergence as a problem: 'What is wrong with you?' 'Why can you not just be normal?' These words, even in frustration, are internalised and carried for life.

Find their thing. Every neurodivergent child has something - a passion, an interest, an activity – that lights them up. It might not be academic. It might not be conventional. It might be Minecraft, dinosaurs, a specific type of music, or an obscure hobby that you know nothing about. This is not just a nice-to-have. For a child whose self-esteem is being battered by the daily challenges of school and social life, their special interest or passion can be the anchor that sustains their sense of self.

Build a support network for yourself. Parenting a neurodivergent child can be isolating, particularly if the people around you do not understand neurodivergence. Other parents of neurodivergent children, online communities, local support groups, and your own therapy or supervision can provide the understanding, validation, and practical advice that you need. You cannot pour from an empty cup, and your wellbeing directly affects your capacity to support your child.

Accept that you will get it wrong sometimes. You will lose patience. You will say the wrong thing. You will make decisions that, in hindsight, you would have made differently. This does not make you a bad parent. It makes you a human parent of a child with complex needs. Repair - acknowledging when you got it wrong and reconnecting - is more important than perfection.

Finally, look after your own mental health. Many parents of neurodivergent children discover, during the process of supporting their child, that they are neurodivergent themselves. This is not uncommon - neurodivergence has a strong genetic component. If you recognise yourself in your child's experience, that is worth exploring, both for your own sake and because understanding your own brain helps you understand theirs.
