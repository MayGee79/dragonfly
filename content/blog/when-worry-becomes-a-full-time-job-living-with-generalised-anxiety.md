Some people have anxiety that arrives in peaks - a panic attack, a specific phobia, a wave of social dread before an event. It comes, it is terrible, and then it passes.

Generalised anxiety is different. It does not come and go. It moves in and takes up permanent residence.

Living with GAD is like having a background programme running on your brain at all times, using up processing power that you need for everything else. While you are making breakfast, part of your mind is calculating whether you can afford the car repair. While you are in a meeting, part of your mind is wondering whether your child seemed upset this morning. While you are trying to sleep, your mind is reviewing every conversation from the day and previewing every possible problem tomorrow might bring.

The content of the worry shifts constantly. This is one of the things that distinguishes GAD from other anxiety presentations. It is not anchored to one fear. It is free-floating, and it will attach itself to whatever is available. Resolve the financial worry and the health worry takes over. Reassure yourself about the health worry and the relationship worry steps in. People with GAD sometimes describe it as a game of whack-a-mole - knock one worry down and another pops up immediately.

This happens because the underlying issue in GAD is not the specific content of the worry. It is intolerance of uncertainty. People with generalised anxiety have difficulty tolerating not knowing. The ambiguity is the threat. 'What if' is the most loaded phrase in their vocabulary, and the answer 'I do not know' is almost unbearable.

This drives a range of behaviours that look productive but are actually anxiety management: excessive planning, list-making, researching, seeking reassurance, asking 'what if' questions, over-preparing for every eventuality, difficulty delegating (because nobody else will do it thoroughly enough), and decision paralysis (because every decision carries the possibility of being wrong).

Reassurance-seeking deserves particular attention because it is so common and so frustrating for everyone involved. The person with GAD asks for reassurance: 'Do you think it will be okay?' They receive it: 'Yes, it will be fine.' They feel briefly better. Then the doubt creeps back: 'But what if they were just saying that? What if they do not know? What if there is something they have not considered?' So they ask again, or they ask someone else, or they Google it. The relief from reassurance has a shorter and shorter half-life.

Living with GAD is exhausting. The mental energy consumed by constant worry leaves little for concentration, creativity, patience, or pleasure. People with GAD often describe feeling tired all the time, being irritable, having difficulty concentrating, and finding it hard to enjoy things even when nothing is actively wrong. They know their worrying is excessive - this is not a lack of insight. They simply cannot stop.

Sleep is often severely affected. The transition from wakefulness to sleep requires a degree of letting go that is extremely difficult when your brain is running a threat-assessment programme. Many people with GAD describe their mind 'switching on' the moment their head hits the pillow, or waking at 3am or 4am with a fully formed worry that will not release them.

So what helps? The evidence base for treating GAD is strong. Cognitive behavioural approaches work on identifying and challenging worry patterns, testing predictions against reality, and gradually building tolerance of uncertainty through behavioural experiments. Rather than trying to achieve certainty (which is impossible), the work focuses on building the capacity to sit with not knowing.

Worry time is a simple but surprisingly effective technique: designating a specific 15-minute window each day for worry, and postponing worries that arise outside that window. This is not about suppressing worry - it is about containing it. Many people find that when they sit down for their designated worry time, the worries feel less urgent than they did when they first appeared.

Body-based approaches matter too, because GAD keeps the nervous system in a state of chronic low-level activation. Regular exercise, diaphragmatic breathing practice, progressive muscle relaxation, and mindfulness meditation (specifically the kind that builds present-moment awareness rather than the kind that asks you to empty your mind, which is a form of torture for someone with GAD) all help regulate the physiological component.

Perhaps most importantly, therapy for GAD works best when it addresses the relationship with uncertainty itself, rather than trying to resolve each individual worry. The goal is not a life without worry - that does not exist for anyone. The goal is a life where worry takes up a proportionate amount of space rather than all of it.
