You have probably seen the social media posts. 'ADHD is a superpower.' 'Autism is my greatest gift.' The intention is good - to counter the relentlessly negative narrative that has surrounded neurodivergence for decades. But the framing creates its own problems.

If ADHD is a superpower, then why am I struggling? If autism is a gift, then why does it hurt? The toxic positivity of the 'superpower' narrative invalidates the real difficulties that neurodivergent people face, and it creates a new kind of pressure: not just to cope, but to thrive, to excel, to turn your neurodivergence into a competitive advantage. For people who are drowning, being told they have superpowers is not empowering. It is the opposite.

But the opposite narrative - pure deficit, disorder, dysfunction - is also damaging and incomplete. The reality is more nuanced than either extreme, and the nuance matters.

Neurodivergent brains do have genuine strengths. These are not compensations or consolation prizes. They are real cognitive and creative capacities that arise from the same neurological differences that create the challenges. ADHD brains often excel at divergent thinking - generating novel connections and ideas. They can hyperfocus on topics of genuine interest with an intensity that neurotypical brains rarely achieve. Many people with ADHD are highly creative, energetic, spontaneous, and able to think quickly under pressure.

Autistic brains often bring extraordinary attention to detail, pattern recognition, systematic thinking, deep subject expertise, loyalty, honesty, and a capacity for sustained focus on areas of interest that produces genuine mastery. Many autistic people have a strong sense of justice and fairness that, while sometimes rigid, also makes them principled and trustworthy.

The key word in all of this is 'often.' Not all neurodivergent people have the same strengths, just as not all neurotypical people have the same strengths. Neurodivergent strengths are tendencies, not guarantees. And they coexist with genuine difficulties that cannot be willed away by a positive attitude.

The strengths-based approach I use holds both truths simultaneously. Yes, your ADHD brain could generate more ideas in ten minutes than most people generate in a day. And yes, your ADHD brain makes it genuinely difficult to follow through on any of them. Both are real. Neither cancels the other out.

There is a deeper complexity here too. Sometimes the same trait is both a strength and a challenge, depending on the context. Autistic attention to detail is an asset in quality control and a challenge when it becomes rigid perfectionism. ADHD spontaneity is an asset in creative brainstorming and a challenge when it manifests as impulsive decision-making. Hyperfocus is a gift when directed at something productive and a problem when it means you forgot to eat, missed three appointments, and lost four hours to something that was not important.

A genuinely helpful strengths-based approach does several things. It validates the strengths without minimising the challenges. It helps the person identify their specific strengths (not generic 'neurodivergent superpowers' but their actual, individual capacities). It explores the contexts in which those strengths are most useful and the contexts in which they become liabilities. And it develops strategies for leveraging strengths whilst managing the difficulties - not instead of managing them.

For parents of neurodivergent children, the strengths-based approach is particularly important. A child who grows up hearing only about what they cannot do will build an identity around deficit. A child who grows up hearing only about their superpowers will be unprepared for the real difficulties. The balanced approach - 'Your brain does some things brilliantly and some things are harder, and we are going to work with both' - gives them the most solid foundation.

If you are neurodivergent and someone tells you that your neurodivergence is a superpower, you are allowed to disagree. And if someone tells you it is purely a disorder, you are allowed to disagree with that too. Your experience is more complex than either narrative allows for. Both the strengths and the struggles are real, and both deserve acknowledgment.
