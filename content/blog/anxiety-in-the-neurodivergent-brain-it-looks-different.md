Standard anxiety psychoeducation - the kind I have been writing about over the past few weeks - is a useful foundation. But if you are neurodivergent, you may have been reading those articles thinking 'yes, but...' Because anxiety in the neurodivergent brain often looks different, feels different, and responds to different things.
The underlying mechanisms, the triggers, and the maintaining factors can be fundamentally different for people with ADHD, autism, and other forms of neurodivergence. And mainstream anxiety treatment, designed largely for neurotypical presentations, often misses this.
Let me start with ADHD. Anxiety and ADHD co-occur at remarkably high rates - some studies suggest up to 50% of people with ADHD also meet criteria for an anxiety disorder. But the relationship is more complex than simple co-occurrence. ADHD creates the conditions for anxiety to thrive.
When you have ADHD, you live with a near-constant experience of things going wrong in ways you cannot predict or prevent. You forget important things. You miss deadlines. You lose your keys, your wallet, your train of thought mid-sentence. You arrive late despite leaving early. You say the wrong thing at the wrong time. Over years, this accumulates into a deep, justified anxiety about your own reliability. You learn to expect failure, because failure has been your lived experience, often through no fault of your own.
This is not irrational anxiety. It is a rational response to a genuinely unpredictable experience. And this matters therapeutically, because telling someone with ADHD to 'challenge their anxious thoughts' does not work when the anxious thoughts are often accurate predictions based on past experience. The work needs to include practical ADHD management alongside anxiety treatment.
Then there is rejection sensitive dysphoria - the intense emotional pain triggered by perceived rejection or criticism that is common in ADHD. RSD is not just social anxiety, though it can look like it. It is a neurological sensitivity to rejection cues that produces an emotional response far out of proportion to the situation. A friend not replying to a text can feel devastating. A mildly critical comment can produce hours of rumination. The anticipation of possible rejection can lead to avoiding relationships, opportunities, and risks altogether.
Autistic anxiety has its own distinct patterns. Sensory overload is a major anxiety trigger that is entirely absent from standard anxiety models. Fluorescent lights, background noise, unexpected touch, strong smells, busy visual environments - these are not minor irritations for many autistic people. They are genuine threats to regulation, and the anxiety they produce is a rational nervous system response to an environment that is genuinely overwhelming.
Uncertainty and unpredictability are particularly anxiety-provoking for many autistic people, but for different reasons than in GAD. In GAD, the person worries about what might happen. In autistic anxiety around uncertainty, the distress is often about not having enough information to prepare, to predict, or to know what will be expected of them. Changes to routine, ambiguous social situations, unclear instructions, open-ended plans - these can produce intense anxiety because they remove the frameworks that help the autistic person navigate the world.
Demand avoidance - sometimes associated with the PDA profile of autism - is another anxiety driver that looks very different from typical avoidance. Here, the anxiety is triggered by demands and expectations themselves, including demands the person wants to meet. The nervous system perceives demands as threats, producing avoidance, resistance, and distress that can be mistaken for defiance or laziness.
Masking - the process of suppressing neurodivergent traits to appear neurotypical - is itself a major source of anxiety. The cognitive load of monitoring your behaviour, scripting conversations, suppressing stims, maintaining eye contact, and performing social norms is enormous. Many neurodivergent people live in a state of perpetual performance anxiety, not because they fear social judgment in the way someone with social anxiety might, but because the cost of dropping the mask feels dangerous.
What does this mean for treatment? First, it means that anxiety in neurodivergent people often requires a different approach. Standard CBT protocols may need significant adaptation. Challenging anxious thoughts does not work when the thoughts are based on legitimate past experience. Graded exposure needs to account for sensory and demand-related triggers. Relaxation techniques need to be offered in neurodivergent-friendly forms (many autistic people find guided meditation aversive, for instance).
Second, it means that treating the anxiety without addressing the underlying neurodivergence is like mopping the floor without turning off the tap. ADHD management, sensory accommodations, reduction of masking pressure, communication adjustments - these are not extras. They are foundational.
Third, it means finding a therapist who understands neurodivergence is not a luxury - it is a necessity. A therapist who does not understand masking may interpret a client's composed presentation as evidence that they are 'not that anxious.' A therapist who does not understand sensory processing may dismiss environmental triggers as avoidance. A therapist who does not understand RSD may treat it as standard social anxiety and wonder why their interventions are not working.
If you are neurodivergent and anxious, your anxiety is valid, it makes sense in context, and it is treatable - but it needs to be treated as the specific thing it is, not as a generic anxiety presentation that happens to occur in a neurodivergent person.
