There is a version of you that other people see. The one who makes eye contact, laughs at the right moments, says the right things, sits still, follows the conversation, handles the noise, manages the demands, and appears, by every external measure, to be coping.

And then there is the version underneath. The one who is exhausted from the performance. Who scripts conversations in advance and conducts post-mortems afterwards. Who suppresses the urge to stim because it looks odd. Who forces eye contact despite it feeling uncomfortable or even painful. Who monitors every social interaction for signs that they are getting it wrong.

This is masking. And for many neurodivergent people, it is not occasional behaviour - it is a way of life.

Masking (also called camouflaging) is the process of consciously or unconsciously suppressing neurodivergent traits and adopting neurotypical behaviours in order to fit in, avoid detection, or reduce negative social consequences. It is common across neurodivergent profiles - autistic masking has received the most research attention, but ADHD masking and compensation are equally significant.

Masking might look like rehearsing and scripting social interactions. Mimicking other people's facial expressions, body language, and conversational patterns. Forcing yourself to maintain eye contact when it is uncomfortable. Suppressing stims (repetitive movements that help with regulation) in public. Hiding difficulties with noise, light, touch, or other sensory input. Compensating for executive function difficulties with elaborate systems that require enormous effort to maintain. Concealing emotional reactions that feel too intense for the situation. Never saying 'I do not understand' because the cost of appearing confused feels too high.

Why do people mask? Because the alternative - being visibly neurodivergent in a neurotypical world - often carries consequences. Bullying, exclusion, professional disadvantage, relationship difficulties, and the subtle but persistent message that who you actually are is not acceptable. Masking is a survival strategy. It develops early, often before the person is aware they are doing it, in response to social environments that reward conformity and punish difference.

The cost of masking is enormous. The most immediate cost is exhaustion. Masking requires sustained cognitive effort - monitoring your behaviour, suppressing your instincts, performing a version of yourself that does not come naturally. This is like running a background programme that uses up processing power you need for everything else. By the end of the day, there is nothing left.

This is why many neurodivergent people have meltdowns or shutdowns at the end of the day or the end of the week - not because those moments are inherently stressful, but because the mask has consumed all available resources and the nervous system can no longer sustain the performance.

The longer-term cost is identity erosion. When you have been masking for years or decades, you may lose touch with who you actually are underneath. What do you actually enjoy versus what have you learned to perform enjoyment of? What are your real opinions versus the opinions you have adopted because they are safer? What do you actually need versus what you have learned to go without? This identity confusion is one of the most painful aspects of recognising masking, particularly for people diagnosed late in life.

Masking is also a major contributor to neurodivergent burnout. Unlike standard burnout, which is primarily about workload, neurodivergent burnout is about the cumulative cost of operating in a world that was not designed for your brain. Masking accelerates burnout by adding the enormous cognitive cost of the performance to the already elevated demands of navigating a neurotypical environment.

Women and girls are disproportionately affected by masking, which is one reason why neurodivergent conditions are under-diagnosed in women. Girls are socialised to mask from an early age - to be compliant, to be pleasant, to manage relationships, to not cause a fuss. These social expectations align with masking behaviours, making the masking invisible. A girl who is masking her autism may look like she is simply well-behaved and socially competent. A woman who is masking her ADHD may look like she is simply organised and hard-working. The mask is indistinguishable from the expectation.

So what can be done? First, awareness. Recognising that you mask - understanding what it costs, when you do it, and what triggers it - is the beginning of being able to make choices about it rather than doing it on autopilot.

Second, finding safe spaces to unmask. This might be alone, with a trusted partner or friend, in a neurodivergent community, or in therapy. Unmasking is not something that can or should happen everywhere - there are contexts where masking provides genuine protection. But having some spaces where you can be fully yourself is essential for sustainability.

Third, reducing the need to mask. This is systemic as well as individual - advocating for environments that accommodate neurodivergent needs (at work, at school, in social settings) reduces the pressure to mask. It also involves internal work: challenging the belief that who you really are is unacceptable, and building a relationship with yourself that includes your neurodivergent traits rather than hiding them.
