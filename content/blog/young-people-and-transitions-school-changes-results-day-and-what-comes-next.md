Results day. Two words that carry an almost unbearable weight for the young people waiting for them.

Whether it is GCSEs, A-levels, or university results, the days and weeks around results represent one of the most significant transitions in a young person's life. Not just because the results themselves open or close doors, but because results day is a moment of identity formation. Am I clever? Am I good enough? Did I do what was expected? What does this say about who I am?

For a young person who gets the results they hoped for, there is joy and relief - followed surprisingly often by anxiety about the next stage. University acceptance comes with the reality of leaving home, making new friends, navigating independence, and wondering whether they will cope. A good set of GCSEs leads to the pressure of A-level choices. Results are never just results. They are transitions in disguise.

For a young person who does not get the results they hoped for, the experience can feel devastating - particularly in a culture that treats exam results as a definitive verdict on worth and potential. The grief is real. The shame is real. And the adults around them need to resist the urge to immediately fix it with practical solutions (clearing, resits, alternative pathways) before the emotional experience has been acknowledged.

Let them be upset first. Then help them problem-solve.

Beyond results day, the transition from one school phase to another is consistently underestimated in terms of its psychological impact. Moving from primary to secondary school involves leaving behind everything familiar - the building, the teachers, the routines, the friendship groups - and entering an environment that is bigger, louder, more complex, and less nurturing. For anxious children, this transition can trigger significant distress. For neurodivergent children, the loss of familiar routines and the increased social and sensory demands can be overwhelming.

The transition from school to whatever comes next - university, apprenticeship, employment, a gap year, or nothing clear at all - is another underestimated moment. For eighteen years, life has followed a prescribed structure. School starts in September. Holidays happen at fixed times. Progress is measured in grades and reports. And then suddenly, the structure disappears. For some young people, this freedom is exhilarating. For others, particularly those who relied on external structure for regulation, it is terrifying.

The young people who struggle most with post-school transitions are often those who have built their identity entirely around academic achievement. If you are 'the clever one' and your value has always been measured by grades, what happens to your identity in a context where grades no longer exist? This is particularly common in neurodivergent young people who have masked their way through school by over-achieving academically.

What can parents and supporters do? For primary to secondary transitions: start preparation well in advance. Visit the new school multiple times. Walk the route. Meet the form tutor. Create visual guides to the new routine. Acknowledge the anxiety without minimising it. 'This is a big change and it makes sense that you feel nervous about it.'

For results day: manage your own anxiety first. Your child will take emotional cues from you. Celebrate good results genuinely but do not make them the only thing that matters. If results are disappointing, prioritise the emotional response before the practical one. There are always alternative pathways.

For the post-school transition: help them build a sense of identity and purpose that extends beyond academic achievement. Encourage exploration. Tolerate uncertainty (theirs and yours). Accept that 'I do not know what I want to do yet' is a perfectly acceptable position for an eighteen-year-old. And if the transition is genuinely overwhelming them, professional support can help.

The common thread in all adolescent transitions is this: the young person is being asked to let go of something familiar and step into something unknown. Their capacity to do this depends on the safety net around them - the relationships, the validation, and the assurance that they are more than their results, their school, or their current circumstances.
