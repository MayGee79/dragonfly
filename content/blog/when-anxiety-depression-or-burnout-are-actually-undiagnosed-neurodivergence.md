A pattern I see frequently is this: Someone arrives with anxiety, depression, or burnout. They may have had several courses of CBT. They may be on antidepressants that take the edge off but never quite resolve things. They have tried everything the mental health system has offered them, and while some of it has helped a bit, they still feel fundamentally like something is not right.

And then, somewhere in the conversation - sometimes in the first session, sometimes a long time in - a different picture starts to emerge. The person who is 'anxious' is actually spending enormous cognitive energy compensating for executive function difficulties. The person who is 'depressed' is actually experiencing autistic burnout from years of masking. The person who is 'burned out' has been running a neurodivergent brain on a neurotypical schedule for decades, and the system has finally crashed.

The problem is not that the anxiety, depression, or burnout are imaginary. They are real. But they are downstream effects of an unidentified upstream cause - neurodivergence. Treating the anxiety without identifying the ADHD is like treating a fever without identifying the infection. You can manage the symptom, but the underlying condition continues to generate new symptoms.

How does this happen? Several factors converge. First, there is the presentation overlap. Many symptoms of ADHD look like anxiety: restlessness, difficulty concentrating, sleep disruption, irritability. Many symptoms of autistic burnout look like depression: withdrawal, fatigue, loss of interest, low mood. Clinicians who are not looking for neurodivergence may see the surface presentation and diagnose what it most closely resembles.

Second, there are the demographic blind spots. ADHD and autism were historically studied in white boys, and the diagnostic criteria reflect that population. Women, people of colour, and anyone who does not fit the stereotypical presentation are more likely to be missed. Women with ADHD are frequently diagnosed with anxiety or depression instead, because their inattentive, internalising presentation does not match the hyperactive, externalising stereotype. Women with autism are frequently missed entirely because their masking is so effective that the underlying autism is invisible.

Third, there is the issue of access. Getting an ADHD or autism assessment as an adult in the UK is difficult. NHS waiting times for adult assessment can be years. Private assessment costs hundreds of pounds. Many people simply do not have access to the diagnostic pathway, even when the clinical picture strongly suggests neurodivergence.

What are the signs that an anxiety or depression presentation might have neurodivergence underneath? Treatment resistance is the most obvious one - if standard anxiety or depression treatment is not working, or is only partially effective, it is worth asking why. A history of these difficulties that extends back to childhood (even if it was not recognised at the time) is another indicator. Family history of ADHD, autism, dyslexia, or other neurodivergent conditions increases the probability. Executive function difficulties (organisation, time management, task initiation, working memory) that go beyond what anxiety alone would explain. Sensory sensitivities. Social difficulties that are not just shyness. A sense of being different that the person has carried their entire life but never quite been able to articulate.

I am not suggesting that everyone with anxiety or depression is secretly neurodivergent. I am suggesting that for some people, neurodivergence is the missing piece that explains why standard treatment has not worked, and that it is worth exploring.

If any of this resonates, the first step is to talk to a clinician who understands neurodivergence - whether that is your GP, your therapist, or a specialist. Many people find it helpful to do some self-education first: reading about ADHD and autism (particularly from neurodivergent authors), watching content created by neurodivergent people, and seeing whether the descriptions match their experience. Self-identification is a valid starting point, even if formal assessment is the eventual goal.

And if you are a therapist reading this: please consider neurodivergence as a possible factor when clients present with treatment-resistant anxiety, depression, or burnout, particularly in women and particularly when the history suggests lifelong rather than situational difficulties. The question is not 'Does this person have anxiety?' - they probably do. The question is 'What is driving the anxiety?' - and the answer might be neurodivergence.
