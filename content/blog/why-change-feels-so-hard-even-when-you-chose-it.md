You wanted this change. You planned for it, worked towards it, maybe even longed for it. So why does it feel so hard now that it is happening?

This is one of the most common and most confusing experiences of being human. The change you chose - the new job, the move, the relationship, the retirement, the baby, the decision to leave - is here. And instead of the excitement and relief you expected, you feel lost, anxious, irritable, or numb. You might even feel grief, which makes no sense to you because you are not supposed to grieve something you wanted.

But you are, because change - any change, even wanted change - involves loss. When you move to a new city, you lose the familiarity of the old one. When you start a new job, you lose the competence and identity you had in the previous role. When you become a parent, you lose the freedom and spontaneity of your previous life. When you retire, you lose the structure, purpose, and social connections that work provided. These losses are real, even when the change that caused them is welcome.

William Bridges, who wrote extensively about transitions, made a useful distinction between change and transition. Change is the external event - the new job, the move, the divorce, the diagnosis. Transition is the internal psychological process of adapting to that change. Change happens relatively quickly. Transition takes much, much longer.

Bridges described three phases of transition: an ending, a neutral zone, and a new beginning. The ending is the loss of what was. The new beginning is the eventual emergence of what will be. And the neutral zone is the uncomfortable, disorienting space in between - where the old identity no longer fits but the new one has not formed yet.

The neutral zone is where most of the difficulty lives. It is the period where you feel like you are between stories. The old narrative about who you are no longer applies, and the new one has not been written yet. This in-between state is profoundly uncomfortable for most people. We want to know who we are, where we belong, and what our life means. The neutral zone refuses to provide those answers, and the uncertainty is hard to sit with.

There is also the issue of identity disruption. Many life transitions involve a fundamental shift in how you see yourself. When you lose a job, you do not just lose an income - you lose a role, a routine, a social identity, and a sense of competence. When your youngest child leaves home, you do not just lose their daily presence - you lose a central organising principle of your life. When a relationship ends, you lose not just the person but the future you had imagined with them.

These are not small things. They are identity-level changes, and they require a kind of grieving and reconstruction that takes time and cannot be rushed.

Why do other people seem to handle change better? The short answer is: they probably do not. People are remarkably good at presenting a composed exterior during transitions while experiencing significant internal upheaval. Social media amplifies this - everyone else's life changes look smooth and exciting, while yours feels messy and frightening. This is an illusion.

The longer answer is that people's responses to change are shaped by their attachment history, their previous experiences of transition, their current support network, their nervous system's baseline state, and whether the change was chosen or imposed. Some people are genuinely more comfortable with uncertainty than others - and for neurodivergent people, transitions can be particularly challenging because they often disrupt the routines and predictability that provide regulation.

So what helps? First, name what is happening. Recognising that you are in a transition - that the discomfort you feel is a normal part of the process, not a sign that you made the wrong decision - is enormously helpful. You are not broken. You are in the neutral zone.

Allow grief. Even for wanted changes. Grief is not only for death and disaster. It is the natural response to any significant loss, and transitions are full of losses, even when they are also full of gains. You can be grateful for the new and still sad about what you left behind. Both can be true simultaneously.

Be patient with yourself. Transitions take longer than you think they should. The expectation that you should have 'settled in' by now, that you should be excited, that you should be over the difficult part - these expectations are often unrealistic and create an additional layer of distress on top of the transition itself.

Maintain anchors. When everything is changing, the things that stay the same become important. Regular routines, familiar activities, trusted relationships, places that feel like home - these provide continuity of identity during a period when identity is in flux.

And if the transition is overwhelming you - if you are stuck in the neutral zone, if the grief will not lift, if the anxiety about the future is paralysing, if you cannot find your way to the new beginning - therapy can help. Transitions are a common reason why people seek therapy, and one of the most rewarding to work with.
