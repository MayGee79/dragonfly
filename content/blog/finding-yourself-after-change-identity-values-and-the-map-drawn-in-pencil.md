Over the past eight weeks, I have written about transitions of every kind - career, family, relationships, menopause, grief, young people, and the psychology of change itself. If there is a common thread, it is this: every significant life transition involves a period of identity disruption. The person you were before the change no longer quite fits. And the person you will become afterwards has not fully formed yet.

So how do you find yourself after change?

The temptation is to rush towards answers. To fill the gap with a new relationship, a new job, a new project, a new identity - anything that provides the structure and certainty that the transition took away. And sometimes that is fine. But more often, rushing towards a new identity without processing the loss of the old one leads to a destination that looks right on paper but does not feel right inside.

The alternative is to sit with the questions for a while. Not forever. Not in a passive, directionless way. But in a curious, exploratory way that allows something genuine to emerge rather than something reactive.

This is where values come in. Values are not goals. Goals are specific, achievable, and finite - get the promotion, buy the house, run the marathon. Values are ongoing qualities of action - the kind of person you want to be, the kind of life you want to live, the principles that guide your decisions. Kindness is a value. Achievement is a value. Connection, creativity, integrity, adventure, learning, independence, service - these are all values.

In my practice, I like the metaphor of a map drawn in pencil. A map drawn in ink is fixed, permanent, definitive - and brittle. When life changes direction (and it will), an ink map becomes obsolete. A map drawn in pencil can be redrawn. The lines can be adjusted, rerouted, erased, and redrawn as circumstances change and understanding deepens.

This is not about having no plan. It is about holding your plan lightly. It is about knowing where your compass points (your values) while accepting that the route may change. It is about building a life that is flexible enough to accommodate the inevitable transitions, rather than one that shatters every time the path shifts.

The mapping process involves several steps. First, an honest audit of where you are now - not where you think you should be, but where you actually are. What feels true? What feels empty? What do you miss? What are you relieved to have left behind? This audit often surfaces things that surprise people. The career they thought defined them turns out to be something they are glad to be free of. The relationship they grieve also contained constraints they had not admitted to themselves.

Next, values exploration. If external structures and roles were removed - if you were not someone's partner, parent, employee, or carer - what would matter to you? What kind of person do you want to be in the world? This is different from asking what you want to do, and it is a distinction that matters. Doing follows being, not the other way around.

Then, small experiments. Rather than committing to a new life plan, try things. Take a course. Volunteer. Have conversations with people in fields that interest you. Visit places. Read widely. These experiments are low-stakes ways of testing whether what you think you value actually resonates when you engage with it in practice.

Throughout this process, be compassionate with yourself. Identity reconstruction is slow, non-linear work. There will be days when the new direction feels clear and exciting, and days when you feel completely lost. Both are part of the process. Neither is the whole story.

If you are in the middle of a transition and wondering who you are becoming, I want you to know that this is not a crisis - it is an opportunity. A painful, disorienting, sometimes frightening opportunity. But an opportunity nonetheless. The map is drawn in pencil for a reason. You get to redraw it.
