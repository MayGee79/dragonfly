You have probably heard the word neurodivergent used more in the past few years than in the previous twenty. It appears in newspaper articles, on social media, in school policies, and increasingly in therapy rooms. But what does it actually mean - and why does the language matter?

Neurodiversity is a biological fact. It refers to the natural variation in human brains and nervous systems. Just as there is biodiversity in ecosystems, there is neurodiversity in human populations. Some people's brains are wired in ways that are common and expected - the neurological majority. Others are wired in ways that differ from that majority. The first group are often called neurotypical. The second are called neurodivergent.

Neurodivergent is an umbrella term that includes (but is not limited to) ADHD, autism, dyslexia, dyspraxia, dyscalculia, Tourette syndrome, and some other neurological differences. It is important to understand that 'neurodivergent' is not a diagnosis - it is a descriptor. You cannot be diagnosed as neurodivergent. You can be diagnosed with ADHD, autism, dyslexia, or another specific condition, and that diagnosis places you under the neurodivergent umbrella.

The neurodiversity paradigm - the philosophical framework behind the language - takes a specific position: that neurological differences are natural human variations rather than deficits or disorders. This does not mean that neurodivergent people do not experience real difficulties. They do. But the paradigm argues that many of those difficulties arise from the mismatch between the neurodivergent person and an environment designed for neurotypical brains, rather than from something inherently wrong with the person.

This is not just a semantic difference. It changes how we think about support, treatment, and identity. A deficit model says: there is something wrong with you, and we need to fix it. A neurodiversity-affirming model says: your brain works differently, some of those differences create genuine challenges, and our job is to understand those challenges, adapt the environment where possible, and develop strategies that work with your brain rather than against it.

In my practice, being neurodiversity-affirming means several things. It means I do not treat neurodivergence as a problem to be solved. It means I recognise that masking - the process of suppressing neurodivergent traits to appear neurotypical - is not coping, it is a survival strategy with a significant cost. It means I adapt my therapeutic approach to suit different neurological profiles rather than expecting every client to engage with therapy in the same way. And it means I hold both truths simultaneously: neurodivergence includes genuine strengths AND genuine challenges, and neither cancels the other out.

A common question: is everyone a bit neurodivergent? No. Neurodivergent traits exist on a spectrum, and it is true that some neurotypical people share some individual traits with neurodivergent people. But neurodivergence is not about having one or two traits in common - it is about the overall pattern of how your brain processes information, manages attention, regulates emotions, handles sensory input, and navigates social interaction. There is a qualitative difference between someone who is occasionally forgetful and someone with ADHD whose executive function difficulties affect every area of their life.

Why does any of this matter? Because language shapes how we think about ourselves and each other. When neurodivergence is framed as a deficit, neurodivergent people internalise the message that they are broken. When it is framed as a difference - one that comes with real challenges and real strengths - people can build an identity around who they actually are rather than around what they lack.
