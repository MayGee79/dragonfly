Anxiety does not stay neatly contained. You cannot leave it at the office door or at the threshold of your relationship. It bleeds into the places that matter most - your work and your closest relationships - and it often does its worst damage there, because those are the contexts where the stakes feel highest.

Workplace anxiety is remarkably common and remarkably under-discussed. Not the normal nervousness before a big presentation, but the chronic, grinding anxiety that makes every email feel threatening, every meeting feel like an evaluation, every piece of feedback feel like a verdict on your worth. People with workplace anxiety often describe a constant sense of being about to be 'found out' - that their competence is an illusion and it is only a matter of time before someone notices.

This is imposter syndrome, and while the term has become so widely used that it risks losing its clinical meaning, the experience remains genuinely distressing. People living with imposter syndrome work harder and longer than they need to, not because they are ambitious but because they are terrified. The over-preparation, the checking, the inability to delegate, the reluctance to take credit - these are not signs of humility. They are anxiety management strategies.

Perfectionism at work is anxiety wearing a suit. The person who cannot send an email without reading it four times. Who stays late to redo work that was already good enough. Who takes criticism - even constructive criticism - as evidence of fundamental inadequacy. Perfectionism looks like high standards from the outside. From the inside, it feels like a protection against the catastrophe of being seen as anything less than flawless.

People-pleasing in the workplace is another anxiety-driven pattern. Saying yes to everything, unable to set boundaries, taking on other people's work, never raising concerns, agreeing in meetings and then quietly doing something different because disagreement feels too dangerous. People-pleasers are often the most valued employees precisely because they never say no - but the cost is burnout, resentment, and a growing sense of having lost themselves in the process.

For neurodivergent people, workplace anxiety has additional layers. Open-plan offices present sensory challenges. Unwritten social rules create constant anxiety about getting something wrong. Executive function difficulties make deadlines and multi-tasking sources of genuine distress rather than mere annoyance. And rejection sensitive dysphoria means that workplace feedback - even neutral or positive feedback - can trigger disproportionate emotional responses.

Anxiety in relationships operates through a different but related set of patterns. At its core, relationship anxiety is about threat - the perceived threat of rejection, abandonment, or not being enough for the person you love.

Attachment theory provides a useful framework here. People with anxious attachment patterns tend to be hypervigilant about their partner's emotional state, reading threat into ambiguous signals. A delayed text response becomes evidence of waning interest. A distracted evening becomes evidence of emotional withdrawal. A disagreement becomes evidence that the relationship is failing. The anxiety drives behaviours designed to restore safety - seeking reassurance, initiating contact, monitoring the relationship's temperature - but these behaviours can create the very distance and conflict they were designed to prevent.

Jealousy, possessiveness, and controlling behaviour are sometimes anxiety-driven, although I want to be clear that understanding the anxiety does not excuse harmful behaviour. If your anxiety is leading you to monitor your partner's phone, restrict their social life, or respond to perceived threats with anger or manipulation, the behaviour needs to change regardless of the emotion driving it. Understanding that it is anxiety-driven helps you address the root cause, but it does not make the behaviour acceptable.

On the other side of the spectrum, avoidant patterns in relationships can also be anxiety-driven. The person who keeps emotional distance, who resists vulnerability, who sabotages closeness before it can lead to hurt - this is often a strategy for managing the anxiety of potential loss. If I do not get close, I cannot be hurt. If I leave before you leave me, I control the pain.

Anxiety can also show up as excessive accommodation in relationships - the person who abandons their own needs, opinions, and boundaries in order to maintain harmony. They agree when they disagree. They apologise when they have done nothing wrong. They suppress anger because expressing it might cause conflict. Over time, this creates a relationship in which one person has essentially disappeared, and the resentment that builds underneath eventually erupts or turns inward as depression.

So what helps? In the workplace, the starting point is often recognising that your anxiety is driving your behaviour rather than your standards. The person who stays late every night is not demonstrating dedication - they are managing fear. Once you see this, you can begin to experiment with doing things differently: sending the email after one read instead of four, leaving on time, letting a piece of work be good enough rather than perfect. These experiments feel dangerous, but they provide the corrective experience that anxiety prevents - the discovery that the catastrophe does not happen.

In relationships, the work often involves understanding your attachment patterns and how they interact with your partner's. Anxious-avoidant pairings are particularly common and particularly volatile, because each person's coping strategy triggers the other's anxiety. Therapy - whether individual or couples - can help both partners understand the dynamic and develop more secure ways of relating.

For both contexts, the deeper work is about building a sense of self-worth that does not depend on external validation. When your worth is internally anchored rather than contingent on your manager's approval or your partner's attention, the anxiety loses its primary fuel source. This is not a quick fix - it is the work of therapy, of self-reflection, of gradually building the internal evidence that you are enough, regardless of what your anxiety tells you.
