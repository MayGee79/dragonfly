Nobody tells you that menopause might make you feel like you are losing your mind.

Not in a dramatic, theatrical way. In a quiet, insidious way. The word that is on the tip of your tongue but will not come. The anxiety that arrives from nowhere and will not leave. The rage that erupts at things that would previously have barely registered. The flatness that descends without warning. The nights you lie awake, drenched in sweat, your brain running a highlights reel of every mistake you have ever made.

Perimenopause and menopause are still discussed primarily as physical events - hot flushes, night sweats, weight gain, dry skin. But for many women, the psychological symptoms are far more disruptive than the physical ones. And because nobody warned them, they spend months or years assuming they are developing an anxiety disorder, depression, or early-onset dementia before someone connects the dots.

The hormonal basis of menopausal mood changes is now well-established. Oestrogen and progesterone are not just reproductive hormones - they are neuroactive, meaning they directly affect brain function. Oestrogen modulates serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine - the neurotransmitters most closely associated with mood regulation. As oestrogen fluctuates and declines during perimenopause, mood regulation becomes less stable. This is not weakness, lack of resilience, or a character flaw. It is neurochemistry.

But menopause is not only a hormonal event. It is also a life transition, and it arrives at a point in many women's lives when multiple other transitions are converging. Children are leaving home (or causing the particular stress that teenagers specialise in). Parents are ageing and may need care. Careers are at a point of either acceleration or stagnation. Relationships are being tested by all of the above. And the body is changing in ways that a youth-oriented culture makes hard to accept.

The identity dimension of menopause is under-discussed. In a culture that associates femininity with youth, fertility, and a particular kind of physical appearance, menopause can feel like an identity crisis. The body is no longer doing what it used to do. The face in the mirror does not match the person inside. The invisibility that many women describe in midlife - being overlooked, talked over, no longer seen - compounds the sense of loss.

Confidence often takes a significant hit. Women who were previously assured, capable, and decisive may find themselves doubting their competence, second-guessing decisions, and avoiding challenges they would previously have embraced. This is partly hormonal (oestrogen decline affects cognitive function, particularly word-finding, short-term memory, and concentration) and partly psychological (when your body and mind feel unreliable, confidence erodes).

Relationships are affected in complex ways. Reduced libido (driven by hormonal changes), irritability, fatigue, and mood swings can create distance between partners. The communication challenges are compounded by the fact that many partners do not understand what menopause involves and may interpret the changes as personal rejection or relationship deterioration.

So what helps? Understanding what is happening is the starting point. When you know that the anxiety is perimenopause, not a new anxiety disorder; that the brain fog is hormonal, not early dementia; that the rage is neurochemistry, not a character flaw - the experience becomes less frightening and more manageable.

HRT (Hormone Replacement Therapy) is worth discussing with your GP. The evidence base for HRT's effectiveness in managing menopausal symptoms - including mood symptoms - is strong. The risks that dominated the conversation twenty years ago have been significantly nuanced by subsequent research. This is not medical advice (I am a therapist, not a prescriber anymore), but it is a conversation worth having.

Therapy can address the psychological dimensions that hormones alone do not reach - the identity questions, the grief, the relationship difficulties, the confidence erosion, and the meaning-making that midlife demands. Many women find that menopause is actually a catalyst for important psychological work, a time when the questions about identity, values, and direction that were previously too busy to ask become impossible to ignore.

Talk about it. The silence around menopause is slowly breaking, but many women still suffer alone because they are ashamed, because they do not want to be seen as making a fuss, or because the people around them do not understand. Finding a community - whether that is friends going through the same thing, an online group, or a therapist who understands menopause - can make a profound difference.

Menopause is not the end of something. It is a transition to the next phase of life - a phase that many women describe as freer, more authentic, and more grounded than what came before. But the transition itself needs support, understanding, and compassion. You deserve all three.
