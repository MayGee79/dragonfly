Over the past eight weeks, I have written about what anxiety is, the different forms it takes, the avoidance cycle, the physical symptoms, generalised anxiety, neurodivergent anxiety, and anxiety at work and in relationships. If you have been reading along and recognising yourself, you might be wondering: do I need professional help?

The short answer is: if you are asking the question, the answer is probably yes. Not because you are broken or because your anxiety is the worst I have ever seen, but because asking the question usually means anxiety has started to cost you something - sleep, confidence, freedom, relationships, opportunities, enjoyment, peace.

Here are some signs that anxiety has moved beyond what self-help strategies alone can address. You are avoiding things that matter to you because of anxiety. Not just things that are genuinely dangerous, but things you want to do - social events, career opportunities, travel, relationships, new experiences. Your world is getting smaller and you can feel it shrinking. You are spending more time managing anxiety than living your life. The rituals, the checking, the reassurance-seeking, the planning, the avoidance - these have become a significant part of your daily routine.

Physical symptoms are persistent and interfering. Chronic headaches, stomach problems, chest tightness, sleep disruption, fatigue - if these have been going on for more than a few weeks and your GP has not identified a physical cause, anxiety may be a factor. Your relationships are being affected. You are irritable, withdrawn, dependent on others for reassurance, avoiding intimacy, or unable to be present with the people you love. You have tried self-help and it is not enough. Books, apps, breathing exercises, lifestyle changes - these are valuable, and for some people they are sufficient. If they are not shifting things for you, that is not failure. It is information.

So what does therapy for anxiety actually involve? Let me demystify it. In the first session, I will ask you about what you are experiencing, how long it has been going on, and what has prompted you to seek help now. I will ask about your history - not because I need your entire life story in the first hour, but because understanding the context helps me understand the anxiety. I will explain how I work and what you can expect. There is no couch. There is no being told what to do. There is no judgement.

The therapeutic approach depends on the person and the presentation. I work integratively, which means I draw on different approaches depending on what is needed. For anxiety, this often includes elements of cognitive behavioural therapy (understanding and changing thought patterns and behaviours), person-centred therapy (providing a safe, non-judgmental relationship), psychoeducation (understanding what anxiety is and how it works), body-based approaches (working with the nervous system rather than just the mind), and, for neurodivergent clients, adaptations that account for ADHD, autism, and related profiles.

Therapy is not a quick fix. It is not a course of antibiotics where you take it for a week and the problem resolves. For most people, meaningful change in anxiety takes somewhere between 8 and 20 sessions, although this varies widely. Some people benefit from short-term, focused work; others prefer longer-term therapy that addresses not just the anxiety but the underlying patterns.

How do you find the right therapist? Look for someone who is qualified and registered with a professional body (BACP, UKCP, or BPC in the UK). Read their website and see if their approach and tone resonate with you. The therapeutic relationship - how safe and understood you feel with that person - is the single strongest predictor of therapy outcomes, more important than the specific approach used. Trust your instinct. If something feels off in the first session, it is okay to say so or to try someone else.

A word on access. Therapy costs money, and I know that is a barrier for many people. If private therapy is not financially accessible, your GP can refer you to NHS talking therapies (previously IAPT), which offers evidence-based treatment for anxiety at no cost. Waiting times vary, but the service exists and it helps a lot of people. Some therapists offer reduced-rate slots, and some charities provide free or low-cost counselling.

If you have read this series and recognised yourself, I want you to know: anxiety is one of the most treatable mental health conditions. The evidence base for therapy is strong, and most people see significant improvement. You do not have to have reached crisis point to deserve help. If anxiety is making your life smaller, harder, or less enjoyable than it should be, that is reason enough.
