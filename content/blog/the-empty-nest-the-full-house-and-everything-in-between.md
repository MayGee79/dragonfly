Nobody warns you about the silence. Your young person has left for university. The house that was full of noise, mess, arguments, laundry, and life is suddenly, profoundly quiet. You wanted this. You worked towards it for eighteen years. And now it is here, and you are standing in the kitchen wondering what you are supposed to do with yourself.

The empty nest is one of those transitions that our culture treats as a non-event. Your children grew up and left home - that is what is supposed to happen. Congratulations, you did your job. Now enjoy your freedom. But for many parents - and perhaps particularly for mothers, who are still disproportionately likely to have organised their identities around their children - the empty nest is a genuine grief experience.

You are not grieving because your child left. You are grieving the end of a phase of life that defined you. The daily routine of caring for children provided structure, purpose, and identity. When that structure is removed, the questions that it was keeping at bay start to surface: Who am I without this role? What do I want from the next chapter? What about my relationship, which has been organised around co-parenting for so long that I am not sure what it looks like without children in it?

These are not small questions, and they deserve more than 'enjoy your freedom.'

At the other end of the spectrum is the full house. Adult children who have returned home after university, after a relationship breakdown, during a housing crisis, or because the economy has made independent living unaffordable. The parent expected an empty nest and got a full one. The dynamics are complicated: the adult child is not a child, but they are in a child's room in a child's house. Boundaries blur. Resentment builds. Both parties feel stuck.

Then there is the sandwich generation - people caring simultaneously for their own children and for ageing parents. This is one of the most exhausting, under-recognised positions in modern family life. You are pulled in multiple directions, trying to meet competing needs with finite energy and time. The guilt of never doing enough for anyone is constant. Your own needs get pushed to the bottom of the pile because everyone else's seem more urgent.

Caring for ageing parents brings its own particular grief. The role reversal - becoming the carer for the person who cared for you - is disorienting. Watching a parent's capabilities decline involves a series of small losses that accumulate over time: the loss of the parent as you knew them, the loss of the relationship as it was, and eventually, the anticipatory grief of knowing what is coming.

Blended families, step-parenting, and the reconfiguration of family after separation or divorce add further layers of complexity. Building new family structures while honouring the feelings of children who did not choose any of this requires patience, flexibility, and emotional skill that nobody teaches you.

So what helps across all of these family transitions? First, name the loss. Even when the transition is positive (your child going to university), even when it is expected (your parents ageing), there are losses embedded in it, and those losses need to be acknowledged rather than minimised.

Invest in the relationship you have with yourself. If your identity has been built primarily around your role as parent, carer, or partner, a family transition that changes that role will feel existential. Building a sense of self that exists independently of your family role - through interests, friendships, work, creativity, or personal development - provides stability when the family structure shifts.

Communicate honestly with the people around you. If your adult child has moved back home, have explicit conversations about expectations, boundaries, and timescales. If you are in the sandwich generation, ask for help - from your partner, your siblings, your friends, professional carers. If you are struggling with the empty nest, tell someone. The shame of finding it hard when you are 'supposed' to be happy keeps too many people silent.

Seek support early. Family transitions are a core part of what therapy addresses, and you do not need to wait until you are in crisis to access it. A few sessions at the point of transition can make an enormous difference to how you navigate what comes next.
