What do you do? It is one of the first questions we ask when we meet someone. And for many people, the answer is not just a description of how they spend their working hours - it is a declaration of identity. I am a doctor. I am a teacher. I am an engineer. I am a manager. The job title is not just what you do. It is who you are.

Which is why career transitions - whether chosen or forced - can feel like an identity crisis. Because they are.

Redundancy is perhaps the most brutal version of this. It is not just a loss of income, although the financial anxiety is real and significant. It is a loss of role, routine, social connection, daily purpose, and professional identity - all at once, often without warning. People who have been made redundant frequently describe feeling invisible, purposeless, and ashamed, even when they know intellectually that the redundancy was not about their performance.

The shame is worth paying attention to, because it is one of the most toxic elements of job loss. We live in a culture that ties worth to productivity. If you are not working, you are not contributing. If you are not contributing, what are you? This narrative is insidious and largely unconscious, and it does enormous damage to people who have lost their jobs through no fault of their own.

Chosen career change carries its own difficulties. The decision to leave a career - particularly a career you trained for, that you are good at, that your identity is built around - is rarely simple. It often involves years of growing dissatisfaction, mounting physical or psychological symptoms (burnout, anxiety, depression), and the gradual, painful recognition that the life you built no longer fits the person you have become.

I know something about this personally. I left a career in medicine - a career I had trained for over a decade to enter - because it was taking it’s toll. The transition was one of the most difficult things I have ever done, not because I regretted leaving, but because the question 'who am I if I am not a doctor?' had no easy answer. The neutral zone lasted much longer than I expected, and it was disorienting in ways I had not anticipated.

Retirement is a career transition that our culture frames as positive but that many people find profoundly difficult. The freedom that retirement promises can feel like emptiness when the structure, purpose, and social connections of work are suddenly absent. People who retire without having developed a clear sense of identity and purpose beyond their professional role are particularly vulnerable to depression and loss of meaning.

Midlife career change has its own particular flavour. At midlife, you are often making career decisions against a backdrop of competing responsibilities (mortgage, children, ageing parents), declining tolerance for risk, and the awareness that time is finite. The question shifts from 'what do I want to be when I grow up?' to 'what do I want to do with the time I have left?' - and that question, while liberating, can also be terrifying.

So what helps when your professional identity is in transition? First, allow the grief. You are not just changing jobs - you are losing a version of yourself, and that loss deserves to be acknowledged. The person who was proud to say 'I am a teacher' needs to grieve the loss of that identity before they can build a new one.

Separate your identity from your role. This is easier said than done, but it is the central therapeutic task. You are not your job title. You are the person who did that job, with all the skills, values, qualities, and experiences that you brought to it. Those do not disappear when the role does.

Reconnect with your values. When external structures fall away, values become the compass. What matters to you? What kind of life do you want to live? What kind of work feels aligned with who you are, not just what you can do? Values-based exploration is central to the work I do with people in career transition.

Be prepared for other people's reactions. Career change, particularly if it involves moving to something perceived as lower-status or less lucrative, can provoke anxiety in the people around you. Their discomfort is about their own relationship with security and identity, not about your decision. You do not need their permission or approval.

If you are in the middle of a career transition and struggling, therapy can provide a space to process the grief, explore the identity questions, and plan the next chapter with support. This is not indulgent - it is practical. Getting the transition right matters, and doing it alone is harder than it needs to be.
