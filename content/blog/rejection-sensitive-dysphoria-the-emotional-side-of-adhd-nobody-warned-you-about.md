If you have ADHD and you have ever had a reaction to criticism, rejection, or perceived disapproval that felt completely out of proportion to the situation - a reaction so intense that it took your breath away, ruined your day, or changed a decision - you may have experienced rejection sensitive dysphoria.

RSD is not an official diagnostic term. You will not find it in the DSM-5 or the ICD-11. But it describes an experience that is so common in ADHD that when people with ADHD hear about it for the first time, the recognition is often immediate and often emotional. 'That is the thing I have never been able to explain.'

Rejection sensitive dysphoria is the experience of intense emotional pain triggered by the perception of rejection, criticism, or failure. Note the word 'perception' - the rejection does not have to be real or intended. A friend cancelling plans can feel like a deliberate exclusion. A neutral email from a manager can feel like a reprimand. A partner mentioning that the kitchen is untidy can feel like an attack on your fundamental adequacy. The intensity of the emotional response is disproportionate to the trigger - and the person experiencing it usually knows this, which adds shame to the pain.

What distinguishes RSD from ordinary sensitivity to rejection? Two things: speed and intensity. RSD responses are fast - almost instantaneous - and they are overwhelming. They are not a gradual build of hurt feelings. They are a flood. People describe them as a physical sensation - a punch to the chest, a dropping in the stomach, a rush of heat. The emotional pain can be so intense that it is indistinguishable from physical pain, and neuroscience research suggests this is not just a metaphor - emotional and physical pain share neural pathways.

RSD affects behaviour in two main ways. The first is emotional flooding: an intense, visible response to the perceived rejection. Tears, anger, withdrawal, defensiveness - often arriving so fast that the person cannot regulate them before they are expressed. This can create conflict in relationships, difficulty in workplaces, and a reputation for being 'too sensitive' or 'overreacting.'

The second is avoidance: restructuring your life to minimise the possibility of rejection. This might look like not applying for jobs or promotions because the possibility of rejection is unbearable. Not starting creative projects because the possibility of criticism is paralysing. Not entering or staying in relationships because the vulnerability of love opens you to devastating rejection. People-pleasing to an extreme degree, saying whatever you think the other person wants to hear to prevent any possibility of disapproval.

This avoidance pattern is often the more damaging of the two, because it is invisible. Nobody sees the job you did not apply for, the relationship you did not pursue, the opinion you did not express, the risk you did not take. RSD quietly makes life smaller, and the person affected may not even recognise it as avoidance because it has been their default for so long.

The current understanding of RSD is that it is related to the emotional dysregulation component of ADHD. ADHD is not just about attention and hyperactivity - it fundamentally affects emotional regulation. The ADHD brain has difficulty modulating emotional responses, meaning emotions arrive faster, hit harder, and take longer to regulate. RSD appears to be a specific manifestation of this broader emotional dysregulation, with a particular sensitivity to cues that signal rejection, criticism, or failure.

So what helps? First, naming it. Understanding that RSD is a feature of ADHD - not a personality flaw, not dramatic behaviour, not evidence of weakness - is profoundly validating. Many people spend years believing they are fundamentally too sensitive, too reactive, too much. Learning that there is a neurological basis for their experience changes their relationship with it.

Developing awareness of the trigger-response pattern helps create space between the trigger and the reaction. This is not about suppressing the emotion - it is about learning to notice it arriving ('My RSD has been triggered') before it drives behaviour. Naming the experience in the moment can reduce its intensity: 'This feels like rejection, and my response is disproportionate. I need a few minutes before I respond.'

Building a robust sense of self-worth that does not depend on external approval is the longer-term work. When your self-worth is internally anchored, rejection stings but it does not devastate. This is not a quick fix - it is the work of therapy, of self-compassion practice, of gradually building an internal evidence base that your worth is not contingent on anyone else's opinion.

Relationships benefit from the partner understanding RSD. When your partner knows that your intense reaction to a mild comment is RSD rather than aggression or manipulation, they can respond differently - with reassurance rather than counter-attack, with patience rather than frustration.
