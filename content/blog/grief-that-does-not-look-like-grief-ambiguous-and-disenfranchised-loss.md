When someone dies, we have a framework for what to do. There is a funeral. People send flowers. You are expected to be sad. The grief is recognised, validated, and given space - imperfectly, but at least there is a cultural script.

But what about the losses that do not have a funeral? The end of a career. A diagnosis that changes what your body can do. A friendship that fades without a clear ending. The life you expected to have that is not the life you got. A miscarriage. A relationship you chose to leave. A country you left behind. The person your parent used to be before dementia. The child you hoped for who never came.

These are real losses. They produce real grief. But because nobody died, the grief often goes unrecognised - by others and by the person experiencing it.

Pauline Boss coined the term 'ambiguous loss' to describe losses that lack clarity or closure. There are two types. The first is when someone is physically present but psychologically absent - a parent with dementia, a partner with a brain injury, a family member with severe mental illness. They are still alive, still here, but the person you knew is gone. The second is when someone is physically absent but psychologically present - a missing person, a family member you are estranged from, a child who was adopted. They are gone, but the relationship continues in your mind.

Ambiguous loss is particularly cruel because it resists resolution. There is no clean ending, no closure, no point at which the loss is 'done.' The grief cycles and recycles, triggered by reminders, by changes in the person's condition, or by the simple passage of time. People experiencing ambiguous loss often feel stuck - unable to grieve fully because the loss is not final, unable to move on because the loss is real.

Disenfranchised grief is Kenneth Doka's term for grief that is not openly acknowledged, socially validated, or publicly mourned. This includes grief for losses that others do not recognise as significant (a pet, a friendship, a professional identity), grief experienced by people whose relationship to the deceased is not acknowledged (an ex-partner, a colleague, an affair partner), and grief in circumstances where the loss is stigmatised (death by suicide, death by overdose, termination of pregnancy).

When grief is disenfranchised, the person is essentially told - by omission or by explicit dismissal - that their loss does not count. 'It was just a dog.' 'You should be over it by now.' 'At least you can have another baby.' 'But you chose to leave.' These responses, however well-intentioned, invalidate the grief and push it underground, where it does not resolve but festers.

Some of the most painful non-death losses I see in therapy include the loss of health or physical capability (a diagnosis of a chronic illness, an injury that changes what you can do, the gradual decline of your body). The loss of the life you expected - the career that did not materialise, the family you planned that did not happen, the marriage that was supposed to last. Miscarriage and pregnancy loss, which are shockingly common and shockingly under-supported. The end of a friendship, which can feel as devastating as a romantic breakup but lacks any cultural framework for processing. Estrangement from family, where the loss is ongoing and complicated by guilt, anger, and social judgment.

What do all of these have in common? They involve loss. They produce grief. And they often go unrecognised.

So what helps? First and most importantly: give yourself permission to grieve. If you have lost something that mattered to you, you are entitled to grieve it, regardless of whether anyone else recognises the loss as significant. Your grief is not dependent on other people's validation.

Name the loss specifically. Vague distress is harder to process than named grief. 'I am grieving the career I thought I would have' is more workable than 'I feel bad about work.' 'I am grieving the person my mother used to be' is more workable than 'I find visiting difficult.'

Find people who get it. If your social circle is not recognising your grief, seek out people who understand - whether that is a support group, an online community, or a therapist. Being heard and validated is not a luxury in grief. It is a necessity.

Be patient with the timeline. Grief does not move in a straight line, and for ambiguous losses it may cycle repeatedly. There is no 'should' about how long it takes. If you are still grieving something that happened years ago, that is not pathological - it is human.

And if the grief is stuck - if you are unable to function, unable to feel anything, or unable to stop feeling everything - therapy provides a safe space to process what you have lost, what it meant, and what comes next.
